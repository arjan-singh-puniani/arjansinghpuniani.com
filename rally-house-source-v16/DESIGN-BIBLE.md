# Rally House: Living Club Design Bible

## Product promise

Rally House is a small tennis academy that feels worth visiting even when the player has no task to finish.

The core fantasy is simple:

> I know this place. I know these people. I can help them improve, shape the academy, and then sit down and watch life continue without me.

The game should earn return visits through attachment, curiosity, mastery, expression, and sensory pleasure. It should not use pressure, loss aversion, streak anxiety, loot boxes, fake scarcity, or punishment for taking a break.

## What makes the loop satisfying

### 1. Immediate tactile feedback

Every meaningful click should produce a visible or audible response within a fraction of a second. Characters turn, gesture, move, speak, or change posture. Rackets, cups, balls, UI surfaces, and placement previews respond visibly.

### 2. A world that moves without the player

Members have schedules, destinations, activities, and small autonomous story beats. The player can stop giving commands and still see new behavior.

The spectator test is mandatory: if the player watches for several minutes, the club should still feel inhabited.

### 3. Relationships that remember

Relationships are not one generic friendship number. Rally House stores familiarity, warmth, trust, rivalry, and small memories. Relationship labels change gradually from “New face” toward “Trusted person.”

The emotional reward is recognition. A member should eventually feel like a person the player knows rather than an icon that yields currency.

### 4. Meaningful competence

Coaching choices should have understandable consequences. Mika’s current problem is forehand preparation. A drill aimed at forehand timing produces a stronger result than a generic drill.

Progress is visible, persistent, and tied to something the player did intelligently.

### 5. Safe experimentation

Decorating, racket setup, social choices, camera movement, and coaching allow experimentation without catastrophic failure. The game should invite “what happens if I try this?” rather than make the player fear wasting resources.

### 6. Player-authored stories

Rally House supplies people, spaces, schedules, preferences, and systems. The player supplies the story.

Examples:

- Leo keeps ending up near the bonsai.
- Nia remembers June’s drink.
- Mika’s forehand preparation finally clicks.
- The player puts a bench in a corner that becomes a favorite observation spot.

These are more valuable than canned plot exposition.

### 7. Gentle short-term direction

Each day has three optional “gentle intentions.” They provide direction without creating obligation.

There is deliberately:

- no streak
- no reset punishment
- no daily-login chest
- no expiring reward
- no notification pressure

Finishing all three grants a modest reward and then explicitly releases the player from task pressure.

### 8. Long-term identity

The persistent arc is not “numbers go up.” It is:

- I became a better coach.
- Mika became a better player.
- Leo and I became close club friends.
- I changed this room.
- This racket is mine.
- I remember when this moment happened.
- The club looks different because I spent time here.

## Visual language

Target: crafted miniature architectural model.

Use:

- warm cream and paper tones
- sage greens
- light and dark woods
- dusty peach
- muted blue-green
- restrained gold
- soft silhouettes
- rounded furniture
- layered architectural trim
- tiny readable clutter
- contact shadows
- small pools of warm light
- visual motion that rewards watching

Avoid:

- generic low-poly emptiness
- harsh black shadows
- saturated mobile-game UI
- giant floating labels
- excessive particle effects
- photorealism that breaks the miniature style

## Animation rule

Animation exists to reveal intention.

A character should visibly communicate:

- where they are going
- who they are attending to
- whether they are relaxed, coaching, talking, drinking, watching, or hitting
- whether a tennis contact succeeded or failed

Tiny idles matter because they prevent characters from reading as props.

## Simulation rule

Prefer a few interacting systems over many isolated features.

A strong feature changes several things at once. A coaching lesson can affect:

- court use
- character destinations
- animation
- Mika’s skill progress
- coach experience
- relationship memory
- money
- the scrapbook

This produces emergence and makes the world feel coherent.

## Ethical engagement rule

Optimize for “I want to come back” rather than “I am afraid to stop.”

Do not add:

- variable-ratio paid rewards
- coercive streaks
- artificial waiting gates
- energy timers
- dark-pattern notifications
- loot boxes
- fake social pressure
- punishment for absence

The strongest retention mechanism should be affection for the place and curiosity about its people.
